How is solving an R-C series circuit different from R-L series?	Same procedure. Swap Xl→Xc, VARsL→VARsC, and the current LEADS instead of lags. (Intro)
In a pure capacitive circuit, does current lead or lag?	Leads the voltage by 90°. (Review Q1)
In an R-C series circuit, by how much are V and I out of phase?	Between 0° and 90°, with current LEADING.
What sets the phase angle in R-C series?	The ratio of R to Xc (tan θ = Xc/R) — the book says "capacitance", but it's capacitive reactance.
In series R-C, does a bigger capacitor make the circuit more or less resistive?	MORE resistive — a bigger C has less Xc, so the angle shrinks. (Counter-intuitive)
What is the same everywhere in a series R-C circuit?	The current: Iₜ = Iᵣ = Ic.
Phase of Eᵣ and Ec relative to the current?	Eᵣ in phase with I; Ec lags I by 90° (i.e., I leads Ec). (So Eᵣ and Ec are 90° apart)
Which three groups organize R-C circuit quantities?	Resistive (R, Eᵣ, Iᵣ, P), capacitive (Xc, Ec, Ic, VARsc, C), total (Z, Eₜ, Iₜ, VA, PF, θ).
Golden rule for Ohm's law in R-C circuits?	Only inside one group; cross groups with the right triangle.
Impedance formula for series R-C?	Z = √(R² + Xc²) (R = √(Z² − Xc²), Xc = √(Z² − R²))
R = 12 Ω, Xc = 16 Ω. Z and I on 240 V?	Z = √(144 + 256) = 20 Ω; I = 240/20 = 12 A. (Main example)
Why isn't Z = 12 + 16 = 28 Ω?	R and Xc are 90° apart, so they add as vectors.
Z = 84 Ω, R = 32 Ω. Xc?	√(84² − 32²) ≈ 77.7 Ω (Review Q4)
20 Ω resistor + 110.5 µF at 60 Hz. First step?	Convert C to Xc: 1/(377 × 0.0001105) ≈ 24 Ω. Then Z = √(20² + 24²) ≈ 31.2 Ω. (Review Q2)
Xc = 50 Ω at 60 Hz. At 1000 Hz?	50 × 60/1000 = 3 Ω (Review Q5)
Voltage drop formulas in series R-C?	Eᵣ = I × R; Ec = I × Xc (Example: 144 V and 192 V)
Capacitance from Xc in the main example?	C = 1/(377 × 16) = 165.8 µF (21–6)
Total voltage from the drops?	Eₜ = √(Eᵣ² + Ec²) (Ec = √(Eₜ² − Eᵣ²))
Meters read 144 V on R and 192 V on C on a 240 V supply. Problem?	No — the drops are 90° apart: √(144² + 192²) = 240 V.
When checking a series capacitor's voltage rating, which voltage do you use?	The capacitor's own drop Ec (× 1.414 if rated in VDC) — not the supply. (Review Q6)
True power formula in R-C series?	P = Eᵣ × I (resistive values only) = 144 × 12 = 1728 W
Reactive power formula?	VARsc = Ec × I = 192 × 12 = 2304 VARs
Apparent power?	VA = Eₜ × I = 240 × 12 = 2880 VA, or √(P² + VARsc²)
Which part of an R-C circuit produces watts?	Only the resistor. (Summary)
How are the impedance, voltage and power triangles related?	Similar triangles: voltage = impedance × I; power = impedance × I². Same angle θ.
Power factor formulas for series R-C?	PF = P/VA = R/Z = Eᵣ/Eₜ (60% in the main example)
Main example θ?	cos⁻¹(0.6) = 53.13°, current leading.
Other trig ratios for θ in R-C series?	tan θ = Xc/R = VARsc/P; sin θ = Xc/Z = Ec/Eₜ
Same numbers, R-L vs R-C: what's different about PF?	The number is the same; the direction differs — R-L is LAGGING, R-C is LEADING.
PF = 76%. Phase angle?	cos⁻¹(0.76) ≈ 40.5° (Review Q3)
Eₜ = 460 V and θ = 44°. Ec?	Ec = Eₜ × sin θ ≈ 460 × 0.695 ≈ 320 V (Practice 8)
Example 21–1: R 5 W, C₁ 3.75 VARs, C₂ 5.625 VARs on 42.5 V. VA and I?	VARs add: 9.375. VA = √(5² + 9.375²) = 10.625; I = 10.625/42.5 = 0.25 A.
Example 21–1: Xc and C of each capacitor?	Ec₁ = 15 V → Xc₁ = 60 Ω → 44.2 µF. Ec₂ = 22.5 V → Xc₂ = 90 Ω → 29.5 µF.
Book error: Example 21–1 computes C₁ = 1/(377 × 30 Ω). Fix?	Should be 60 Ω (the answer 44.2 µF is correct for 60 Ω). Also Figs 21–9/21–10 show 3.73 VARs; it's 3.75.
A 2 W, 120 V lamp must run on 480 V with a series capacitor. Voltage across the cap?	√(480² − 120²) = 464.8 V (not 360 V). (Example 21–2)
Capacitor value for that lamp dropper?	I = 2/120 = 0.01667 A → Xc ≈ 27.9 kΩ → C ≈ 95 nF.
Why use a capacitor instead of a resistor to drop voltage?	The capacitor drops voltage with essentially no watts (reactive power); a resistor would waste ~6 W as heat.
Exception to the lamp-dropper calculation?	It assumes the lamp is a constant resistance; a cold filament or an LED doesn't behave that way.
Why can too much leading PF be a problem?	Over-correction raises voltage and can upset generators/regulators. Aim for ~0.95–1.0, not as capacitive as possible.
Where do you meet series R-C circuits on the job?	RC snubbers across contacts/triacs, phase-shift networks, capacitive droppers in pilot lights and small supplies.
RC circuit: 120 V, resistor 25 Ω dropping 65 V. Steps to find C?	I = 65/25; Ec = √(120² − 65²); Xc = Ec/I; C = 1/(377·Xc). (Review Q7)
240 V, 20 A, 3 kW in a series RC circuit. PF and the route to C?	PF = 3000/4800 = 62.5%. R = P/I², Z = E/I, Xc = √(Z² − R²), C = 1/(377·Xc). (Review Q8)
